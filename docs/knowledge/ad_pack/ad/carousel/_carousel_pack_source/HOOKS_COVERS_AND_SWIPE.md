# Hooks, Covers, and Swipe Mechanics

## 1. The cover has one primary objective

**CORE:** make the right viewer decide that the next slide is worth seeing.

The cover is not required to explain the post. It should make the post legible enough to categorize and interesting enough to enter.

A useful cover formula is:

> **Relevant topic + specific angle + visual salience + unresolved value**

Not every cover needs all four at maximum strength, but weak covers usually miss at least two.

---

## 2. Cover hook families observed in the corpus

### A. Contrarian claim
Examples in the corpus:
- “Posting is not marketing.”
- “Not every client is a good fit.”
- “Never explain your process to potential clients.”
- “Growth requires inefficient effort.”

**Mechanism:** belief conflict.

Use when:
- the audience likely holds the default belief;
- you can explain why the reframe is true.

Failure mode:
- contrarian wording with no real disagreement underneath.

---

### B. Mistake / danger hook
Examples:
- “7 Typography Mistakes.”
- “5 Detailing Do’s and Don’ts.”
- “Miami drivers make these mistakes every September.”

**Mechanism:** loss avoidance + self-diagnosis.

Use when:
- the mistake is specific;
- the viewer can plausibly be making it;
- the correction is actionable.

Failure mode:
- generic “mistakes” selected only because the format performs.

---

### C. Utility / outcome hook
Examples:
- “How to plan 30 days of content in 10 minutes.”
- “How to create a collage effect in Canva.”
- “Grid Systems Every Designer Should Know.”

**Mechanism:** explicit usefulness.

Use when:
- the outcome is concrete;
- the carousel can actually deliver enough of it.

Failure mode:
- promising a complete solution and delivering a teaser for a lead magnet.

---

### D. Named system / framework hook
Examples:
- “The One Page System.”
- “The 532 Method.”
- “The One Degree Rule.”
- “The Illusion of Novelty.”

**Mechanism:** compresses complexity into a memorable object.

Use when:
- the system has distinct parts;
- the name improves recall or explanation.

Failure mode:
- inventing a branded name for obvious advice.

---

### E. Quantity / collection hook
Examples:
- “50+ Psychological Hooks.”
- “10 Business Frameworks Every Creator Should Know.”

**Mechanism:** reference value + completion expectation.

Use when:
- quantity is itself useful;
- items are meaningfully distinct.

Failure mode:
- a large number of near-duplicates.

---

### F. Comparison hook
Examples:
- “Good Product vs. Good Branding.”
- ugly vs viral carousel.
- do/don’t slides.

**Mechanism:** contrast reduces cognitive effort.

Use when:
- the comparison clarifies a decision or concept.

Failure mode:
- false binary.

---

### G. Identity / aspiration hook
Examples:
- “How to be dangerously good at carousel design.”
- “Kinda chic to be a graphic designer.”

**Mechanism:** self-concept and group recognition.

Use when:
- audience identity is specific enough.

Failure mode:
- generic motivational phrasing.

---

### H. Curiosity / incomplete statement
Examples:
- “This is not branding.”
- “Packaging that isn’t boring.”
- “The power of storytelling.”

**Mechanism:** incomplete interpretation.

Use when:
- slide 2 resolves enough of the ambiguity to reward the swipe.

Failure mode:
- mystery for its own sake.

---

### I. Visual-only hook
Examples:
- travel photography;
- automotive details;
- product art direction.

**Mechanism:** taste, novelty, beauty, subject interest.

Use when:
- the image itself is sufficient value.

Failure mode:
- forcing a title onto a stronger image.

## 3. The five visual hook mechanisms extracted from the Grow with Alex material

These are useful as **source-derived heuristics**, not laws.

### 1. World
Build a visual feeling or scene around the topic.
- clarity;
- wonder;
- tension;
- atmosphere.

The point is not merely to show the topic; it is to show the feeling associated with it.

### 2. Borrow
Use something already recognizable so the viewer understands faster.
- familiar person;
- product;
- interface;
- object;
- visual archetype.

Useful because recognition reduces decoding time.

### 3. Cut
Use one dominant color/field and a hard-edged subject cutout.

Useful because:
- silhouette reads fast;
- contrast is strong;
- the subject remains legible at small size.

### 4. Object
Put the actual subject or symbolic object in the scene rather than representing it only with typography.

### 5. Shape
Let one word or dominant typographic mass become the visual shape of the cover.

Important synthesis:

> A hook is not only a sentence. It is the total perceptual event that makes the viewer stop and categorize the post.

## 4. Cover layout primitives extracted from the corpus

The Grow with Alex “layout” carousel provides a useful vocabulary. Treat these as primitives, not templates.

### The Stack
- type concentrated in one vertical area;
- image has room above/beside;
- robust default.

### The Band
- solid text band across the image;
- useful when the image is visually busy or type lacks readable negative space.

### The Split
- image on one half, flat color/type on the other;
- useful when the photo cannot comfortably host text.

### The Overlap
- type intentionally overlaps image content;
- needs strong contrast and confident integration.

### The Corner
- type anchored to a corner;
- leaves image dominant.

### The Low Line
- thin type line at bottom;
- image does most of the work.

### The Frame
- type inside a clear container or boundary;
- useful for busy/dynamic imagery.

### The Punch
- one very large word; everything else subordinate.

The generator should choose a primitive based on image geometry, not randomly.

## 5. Cover composition rules

### 5.1 Feed-size test
**CORE:** if the core visual idea disappears at small size, the cover is fragile.

Test at approximately 15–20% of final display size.

At that scale, check:
- can the topic still be categorized?
- is the dominant word/image still obvious?
- do secondary elements collapse into noise?

### 5.2 One dominant focal point
A cover can have many elements, but only one should win.

### 5.3 Contrast must survive compression
Contrast can come from:
- size;
- luminance;
- color;
- texture;
- isolation;
- scale;
- edge sharpness;
- typography weight.

### 5.4 Text should be image-aware
Do not place copy according to a memorized grid while ignoring the photo.

Choose placement based on:
- negative space;
- subject direction;
- face/gaze;
- horizon;
- high-detail regions;
- tonal contrast;
- focal object.

### 5.5 Avoid title redundancy
If image + subtitle already communicate the premise, do not restate it three more ways.

## 6. Slide 2: the validation bridge

A strong slide 2 typically does one of five things:

1. **Problem:** “Here’s why this matters.”
2. **Proof:** “This really works / this really happened.”
3. **Specificity:** “Here is exactly what I mean.”
4. **Map:** “Here are the components / steps.”
5. **Story start:** “Here is what happened next.”

A slide 2 that says only “swipe to learn more” wastes the most valuable continuation slot.

## 7. Swipe continuation mechanisms

### Open loop
Promise something not yet resolved.

### Structural completion
“5 rules,” “8 layouts,” “10 frameworks.” The viewer knows there is a bounded set.

### Progressive specificity
Each slide moves closer to implementation.

### Escalation
The consequences or insight deepen.

### Contrast chain
Each swipe reveals a new wrong/right, before/after, or example.

### Narrative uncertainty
The viewer wants the outcome.

### Visual novelty
The next slide offers a new composition, scale, scene, or object while preserving the same visual world.

### Pattern anticipation
A repeated structure creates a satisfying expectation, then controlled variation keeps it from becoming dull.

---

## 8. Swipe rhythm

A source carousel explicitly argues that the goal is not for every slide to look identical, but to create an experience of swiping. That idea is strongly supported by the broader corpus.

Useful rhythm pattern:
- big statement;
- explanation;
- visual framework;
- short punchline;
- unexpected visual;
- example;
- synthesis.

This does **not** mean every carousel needs maximal variation. Reference libraries can intentionally use repeated card structures.

Use variation when the content benefits from changing attention mode.

## 9. Hook templates: store families, not phrases

The addendum contains many hook templates such as:
- “I was about to quit when this happened…”
- “I spent [X] years doing this the wrong way…”
- “Most people focus on [obvious thing]…”
- “What if I told you…”

Do not make these literal templates the core system.

Instead store the psychological/semantic families:
- reversal;
- confession;
- hidden cause;
- unexpected constraint;
- counterfactual;
- proof through experience;
- consequence;
- specificity;
- transformation;
- challenge to common belief;
- future projection;
- avoided mistake;
- time/money tradeoff;
- diagnostic question.

Then generate fresh wording from the family and the actual topic.

---

## 10. Cover generation algorithm

For each carousel, generate at least 3 cover concepts from **different hook families**.

For each candidate, specify:
- hook family;
- title;
- optional subtitle;
- dominant visual idea;
- layout primitive;
- focal point;
- why the target viewer should stop;
- risk of being generic/clickbait.

Then score 1–5 on:
- relevance;
- specificity;
- visual clarity;
- curiosity;
- promise-payoff fit;
- originality;
- small-size legibility.

Do not simply choose the most sensational title.

## 11. Two cover tests extracted from the source material

A strong source suggestion was to test the cover in context. Adapted into a more general QC:

### Feed test
Put the cover among real competing posts at feed size.
- does the eye catch it?
- is it distinguishable without zooming?

### Contextless test
Show the cover alone to someone who does not know the topic.
- can they identify what category it belongs to?
- can they state why they might swipe?

The second test should not require the entire message to be understood. It tests category + interest, not full comprehension.
